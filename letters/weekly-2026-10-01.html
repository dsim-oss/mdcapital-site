01

Macro environment

The Fed did the thing the market had talked itself out of in August. On September 16 the FOMC raised rates 25 basis points to 3.75% to 4.00%, on a 12 to 0 vote, its first hike since July 2023. The statement said inflation "remains elevated," and Chair Warsh described the move as removing "a dose of accommodation." The new projections put the median rate at 4.1% at the end of this year and the end of next, and 16 of 19 members see at least one more hike in 2026. As of September 23, futures priced about a 54% chance of another hike at the October meeting.

The data gave the Fed its opening. August payrolls came in at +162,000 against forecasts near 53,000, unemployment held at 4.1%, and June and July were revised up by 55,000 combined. The July print that looked like a cracking job market turned out to be a soft month, not a trend. Headline CPI held at 3.4% year over year, and producer prices ran 5.4%. Oil did the rest. Brent touched about $108 on September 24 on Iran and Strait of Hormuz tensions and Houthi strikes on Saudi Arabia, and it sat near $106 on September 25, with WTI near $96.

The Treasury's long-end buybacks started on September 9 as planned, at up to $4 billion or more per operation. They didn't hold yields down. The 10-year went from 4.74% on August 21 to 5.31% on September 30, its highest since May 2002, and the 30-year reached 5.65%. The odd part is that the last inflation report was soft. August PCE came in at 3.4% against a 3.7% forecast, and core PCE at 3.0% against 3.3%. Futures moved toward a hold at the October 27 to 28 meeting, with the odds of another hike falling to about 35% from about 51% a day earlier. Brent sat near $104 and WTI near $90 on September 30. A hiking Fed, a 10-year above 5%, and oil above $100 is about as hostile a setup as this asset gets, and it's worth remembering that Bitcoin finished the period higher anyway.

02

Bitcoin this period

The period came in three pieces. Bitcoin chopped between $76,000 and $81,000 through early September, and on September 3 it tagged $82,288 on the best ETF inflow day since January, $731 million. Then the middle of the month went wrong in two days. On September 15 the Senate failed to advance the CLARITY Act, 49 to 50, and spot ETFs had their worst day since June at $450 million out. The Fed hiked the next morning. Bitcoin printed the period low of $74,892 that day.

The third piece was the rally. Starting September 18, ETF flows flipped hard, with $433 million in on the 18th, $999 million on the 21st and $715 million on the 22nd. Short positions got squeezed, Strategy disclosed a 950 BTC purchase, and Bitcoin hit $87,447 on September 21. It spent the rest of the week settling between $83,000 and $85,000 and closed September 25 at $84,090. It has held between $83,000 and $85,500 since then and trades at $84,101 this morning. The Fear and Greed index went from 50 on September 17 to 78 on the 22nd and reads 74 today. Bitcoin is now 46% above the June low of $57,650 and 33% below its October 2025 all-time high of $126,198.

What stands out to me is where the buying came from. Last month's rally ran on retail flow while large holders sat out. This one ran through the ETFs and through Strategy itself, against a Fed that was tightening. That's a sturdier base than August, even if the sentiment gauge is running hot again.

03

Featured: the issuer starts buying again

Last letter I told you Strategy was selling Bitcoin, in part, to defend the price of the preferred we own. That was true in early August. Since then it reversed, and I want to lay out exactly how, because two of our three assets sit on that balance sheet.

It bought Bitcoin again. Strategy bought 4,603 BTC in the week of August 24 for $369.7 million, about $80,318 each, paid for with common stock sold through its at-the-market program. It bought another 950 BTC in the week of September 14 at about $79,670, paid for out of cash, and 1,665 BTC in the week of September 21 at about $85,681, for $142.7 million. As of its September 28 filing it holds 847,666 BTC at an average cost of $75,437. Its filings show no Bitcoin sold in this period.

It kept buying back STRC, and it doubled the budget. Across five weekly filings covering August 17 through September 20, Strategy repurchased 7,990,979 STRC shares for $777.8 million, roughly $140 million to $176 million every week, and it bought back another $152 million the week of September 21. On September 8 the board raised the preferred buyback authorization from $1 billion to $2 billion. STRC answered. It has closed above $98 in every session since September 17, and it closed at $99.35 on September 30.

The rate held at 12%. The September 1 filing kept the dividend at 12% for September and repeated that management will keep recommending 12% "until STRC has demonstrated sustained, healthy trading near $100." On September 25 the company also proposed moving STRC and its other preferreds to daily dividends, with the same annual rate. Holders vote on October 28, and the first daily payment would come in early November. On September 30 Strategy said the rate stays at 12% through October.

Where the money came from. Strategy sold about $2.6 billion of common stock through the ATM between August 17 and August 30, then sold none from August 31 through September 20. The buybacks in that stretch were paid from its separate "USD Cash" pool, which fell from $1.61 billion on August 30 to about $1.0 billion. In the week of September 21 it went back to the ATM. Stock sales paid for the 1,665 BTC and $103.9 million of the STRC buyback, and USD Cash covered the other $48.1 million. The roughly $5.0 billion USD Reserve for dividends and interest is barely touched.

Here's how I read it. The company raised equity while its stock traded above the value of its Bitcoin, used it to buy Bitcoin, and is now spending that cash to pull its preferred up toward par. For us, that's the good version of the story. The issuer isn't choosing between the two assets we own right now, it's feeding both. The cost is that the buybacks run about $150 million a week. Last week the common was strong enough to pay for most of that with new stock. If MSTR weakens, the cash pool carries it alone, and it can't do that for long.

07

Risks we're watching

  • A Fed that's hiking. The September hike came with dots that point higher, producer prices running hot, and oil above $100. Bitcoin held up this time. A second or third hike is a different test.
  • Long-end yields above 5%. The Treasury's buybacks were supposed to hold the long end down, and the 10-year rose 57 basis points anyway, to its highest level since 2002. Rising cost of money is the pressure that's capped this asset all year.
  • Issuer concentration. MSTR and STRC are 41% of NAV and sit on one balance sheet. The buybacks defending STRC run about $150 million a week, paid by stock sales when MSTR is strong and by the USD Cash pool when it isn't. If MSTR's premium fades, the support under STRC gets thinner.
  • A flat keystone. Two letters with zero Bitcoin added while the dollar book recovered about $4,400. The fund is fully invested with 2.0% cash and can't stack without a decision or a contribution.
  • Policy. CLARITY failed cloture on September 15 and the next real window is the lame-duck session. Illinois' new Digital Asset Privilege Tax is being challenged in court as of September 10. I'm watching how it ends up applying to the fund's Bitcoin.
  • Derived cash. IBKR cash of $53 and bank cash of $472 carry forward from the ledger and haven't been trued against a statement since August. Neither moves NAV in a meaningful way.

Stepping back, this was the stretch where the fund got back to whole. In May we needed Bitcoin to rally 46% to get your money back. Today we're $389 ahead, after a Fed hike, oil over $100, and a 10-year at its highest since 2002. The June buys did the heavy lifting, and so did an issuer that went back to buying Bitcoin. On the scoreboard that matters most, though, the count hasn't moved since June, and that's the problem I'm working on for the next letter. Dollars are how we settle. Bitcoin is how we keep score.
